You react differently to the same information framed differently.

The framing effect, where choice is influenced by whether information is presented as gains or losses, was famously explored by Tversky and Kahneman in 1981 using the 'Asian disease' scenario (600 lives) rather than a 90%/10% percentage model.

The 90% survival versus 10% mortality framing example is frequently associated with the 1982 study by McNeil, Pauker, Sox, and Tversky regarding the elicitation of preferences for alternative medical therapies.

You respond to the presentation, not just the logic.
